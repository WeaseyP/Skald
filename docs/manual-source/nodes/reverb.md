# Reverb

> Reverb is the sound of a room: thousands of echoes arriving too fast to count, blurring into a tail that fades away after the note stops.

## What it is

Clap your hands in a bathroom and in a cathedral. The clap itself is identical — what changes is everything that arrives *after* it. Sound leaves your hands in every direction, hits walls, floor, ceiling, and comes back to your ears late and quieter. Each of those reflections hits more surfaces and splits again. Within a few tens of milliseconds the reflections stop being countable events and become a continuous, decaying wash. That wash is reverberation, and it is the single strongest cue your brain uses to answer "what kind of space am I in, and how far away is that sound?"

Acousticians measure a room's tail with one number: **RT60**, the time it takes for the sound pressure level to fall by 60 dB after the source is abruptly switched off [Source: https://www.nti-audio.com/en/applications/room-building-acoustics/reverberation-time]. Sixty decibels is a thousandfold drop in amplitude — from "loud orchestral peak" to "roughly the noise floor of a quiet hall". The same source lists real rooms: a recording studio around 0.3 s, a classroom 0.4–0.6 s, a lecture hall 1.0–1.5 s, a concert hall or opera house 1.4–2.0 s, and a stone church anywhere from 2 up to 10 s. Under about 0.3 s a room reads as acoustically "dead"; over about 2 s in a small space it reads as echoey. Those numbers are your mental ruler for the Decay control below.

A real reverb is not one uniform thing. It has three stages, and mix engineers treat them separately. First the **direct sound** reaches you in a straight line. Then the **early reflections** arrive — the first few distinct bounces off the nearest large surfaces, typically within the first tens of milliseconds. They are the part that actually tells you the room's size and shape; strip them out and a sound stops feeling anchored anywhere, even though you cannot consciously hear them as echoes [Source: https://www.waves.com/get-more-from-reverb]. Last comes the **diffuse tail**: the accumulated soup of reflections-of-reflections, dense enough that no individual echo is audible, decaying at the rate RT60 describes. The tail carries the room's character — its size and what its walls are made of.

The gap between the direct sound and the first reflection has its own control name: **pre-delay**. Physically it is just the extra distance the first bounce travelled, so a longer pre-delay implies a bigger room, or you standing closer to the source relative to the walls. Practically, engineers use it to keep a source intelligible: a reverb that starts instantly smears the consonants of a vocal, whereas 20–50 ms of silence first lets the words land dry and the wash arrive behind them. Values between roughly 10 and 60 ms are the common working range, with 30–50 ms typical on a lead vocal; percussive sources usually want little or none, because an audible gap turns into an unnatural "hit, then splash" [Source: https://asmr.education/faq/music-mixing/pre-delay-reverb-purpose-settings-mix-clarity].

Digitally, nobody simulates a room by tracing millions of rays in real time. The classic cheap trick, from Manfred Schroeder in the 1960s, is to build the tail out of **feedback comb filters**: take a delay line, feed a fraction of its output back into its input, and the signal circulates, re-emerging quieter every time round the loop. One comb filter behaves exactly like a pair of parallel walls — and sounds like it, because a single delay reinforces the frequencies whose wavelength fits its loop time and cancels the ones that don't, producing a spiky, metallic, ringing colour. Schroeder's fix was to run four combs in parallel with mutually prime delays of 30–45 ms, then push the result through cascaded allpass diffusers, reaching an echo density approaching the ~1000 echoes per second the ear needs before a tail stops sounding like separate events and starts sounding like a room [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html].

Skald's Reverb node implements the *first half* of that idea and stops there: one feedback comb, no diffusers. That is a real limitation, and it is also the best possible teaching tool, because it lets you hear each ingredient of a reverb by its absence. You will hear the metallic comb colour, you will hear the sparse echo density, and you will hear what "not enough diffusion" actually means — which is knowledge you cannot get from a plugin that hides all of it behind a "Cathedral" preset.

## What it looks like in Skald

Reverb lives in the **Nodes** palette in the left sidebar, listed between Delay and Distortion, with the tooltip "Room tail with decay time, pre-delay and wet/dry mix" (`skald-ui/src/components/Sidebar.tsx:273`). Its node card is deep teal — the colour Skald reserves for space effects (`skald-ui/src/components/Nodes/NodeStyles.ts:96`). Drag it onto the canvas like any other node.

It has exactly two handles:

- **In** — target handle, id `input`, on the left (`skald-ui/src/components/Nodes/ReverbNode.tsx:6`)
- **Out** — source handle, id `output`, on the right (`skald-ui/src/components/Nodes/ReverbNode.tsx:7`)

That is the whole port surface, and the backend enforces it. Reverb is in the "pass-through, no modulation inputs" group alongside Delay, Distortion, Mapper and Output, whose only legal target port is `input` (`skald-backend/core/graph_validate.odin:32,51`). **You cannot wire an LFO into Decay or Mix.** The generator asks for those two parameters with an empty modulation-port name, which switches off the modulation lookup entirely (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:544-545`, and see the guard at `skald-backend/core/param_utils.odin:134-138`). If you want a moving reverb, you move it from the host at runtime through an exposed parameter, not with a patch cable.

Both handles are **audio rate** — one sample in, one sample out, 48000 times a second.

There is one structural fact about Reverb that matters more than its ports. A Reverb node forces a **domain split** in the generated code. Skald normally runs your whole patch once per voice, per sample, so an 8-note chord runs eight copies of your oscillator and filter. A reverb cannot work that way: it owns a single shared delay buffer, and running it inside the voice loop would divide its delay time by the number of active voices, bleed voices into each other's feedback, and cut the tail dead the instant the last note ended. So Skald puts Reverb — and *everything downstream of it* — into a **bus domain** that runs once per sample on the summed output of all voices (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:64-69`, `1874-1876`). Two consequences you will meet in practice:

- **The tail outlives the notes.** The bus block runs regardless of whether any voice is active, so your reverb keeps ringing after the last note releases.
- **Voice-coupled nodes cannot go after it.** Oscillator, ADSR, FM Operator, Wavetable and MIDI Input all read per-voice state, which does not exist on the bus. Wire one downstream of a Reverb and codegen refuses to build, with an error naming the node and telling you to move it upstream (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:89-97`, `1935-1943`). Filter, Gain, Distortion, Panner, Mixer, LFO, Noise and Sample & Hold *are* allowed after a Reverb; they get bus-side state fields instead of per-voice ones (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1134-1157`).

### What you hear as you sweep Decay

Decay is an **RT60 in seconds**, and the generator converts it to a feedback gain for a fixed 75 ms delay tap (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:547,557`). Because that tap length is fixed, Decay is not really "room size" — it is "how many times the same 75 ms echo repeats before it dies", and the number of repeats is simply `decay / 0.075`.

- **0.1 s (minimum).** Feedback gain works out around 0.006 — effectively zero. You hear one 75 ms slap behind the note and nothing after it. This is the setting that exposes the machinery: the "reverb" is audibly a single echo.
- **0.3 – 0.8 s.** Four to eleven repeats. This is the ugly-but-instructive zone. The repeats are still individually countable and the comb resonance is blatant: sustained notes take on a hollow, boxy, faintly metallic ring, and short percussive hits turn into a "boing". This is exactly the artefact Schroeder's parallel combs and allpass diffusers exist to eliminate — you are hearing what one comb sounds like on its own.
- **2 – 5 s.** Twenty-seven to sixty-seven repeats, gain roughly 0.77 to 0.89. The repeats overlap enough that most material stops resolving them as echoes and starts reading as a space. This is the musically useful zone for pads, and it lines up with the real-world hall figures: 1.4–2.0 s for a concert hall, longer for a church [Source: https://www.nti-audio.com/en/applications/room-building-acoustics/reverberation-time]. Note that even at 4 s the tail is only about 13 echoes per second, far below the ~1000/s that reads as genuinely smooth [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html] — so Skald's tail always keeps a little grain and colour. On slow pads that is a feature; on a snare it is not.
- **6 – 10 s (maximum).** Gain 0.92 to 0.95. Cathedral territory, but with a catch that matters: a comb filter with feedback gain *g* settles at roughly **1/(1 − g)** times its input for sustained material. At Decay 6 that is about 12×, and at Decay 10 about 20×. The wet path *gains energy* over time. On a looping pad the wash keeps accumulating until the master soft-limiter squashes everything (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:2415-2418`). You will do this deliberately in the exercise.

The generator hard-caps the feedback gain at 0.95 so it can never reach or exceed 1 and diverge (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:557`). Solving the formula, 0.95 corresponds to a Decay of about 10.1 s — just past the slider maximum, so within the UI the cap never actually bites. It is there to protect against a host calling the setter with a hostile value.

### What you hear as you sweep Mix

- **0.0.** Completely dry. The Reverb node passes the signal through unchanged — but it is still running, still filling its buffer. Turn Mix back up and the tail is already there.
- **0.15 – 0.4.** The working range for most material. The dry signal stays in front and defines the note's attack and pitch; the wet signal sits behind it and defines the space. `pad-sequenced.skald.json` ships at 0.35.
- **0.5 (default).** Equal parts. Already quite far back for anything you want to sound present.
- **1.0.** Fully wet. The dry signal vanishes entirely and you hear only the comb output. Two things happen at once: transients soften (every attack is now 75 ms late) and, at long decay times, the level *rises* rather than falls, because the 1/(1 − g) build-up is no longer diluted by a dry component.

### What "expose" does, and when to use it

Every parameter row in the Parameter Panel has a small link icon next to its label. Clicking it toggles that parameter's membership in the node's `exposedParameters` list (`skald-ui/src/components/ParameterPanel.tsx:197-211,228-236`). A fresh Reverb node ships with **Decay and Mix already exposed** (`skald-ui/src/definitions/node-definitions.ts:127`).

Exposing a parameter changes what the generated Odin looks like. An un-exposed parameter is **baked into the code as a constant** — `0.35` appears literally in the arithmetic. An exposed one becomes a field on the processor struct plus a typed setter, a name-keyed `set_param` / `get_param` dispatch, and a row in the introspectable `<Asset>_PARAMS` table carrying min, max, default and unit. You can see all of this in the checked-in golden file for the reverb fixture: the struct field at `skald-backend/tests/golden/reverb_adsr_exposure.odin.golden:109`, the init at `:127`, the clamping setter at `:260-265`, the PARAMS row at `:332`, and the string dispatch at `:349`.

That is why you would expose Decay and Mix on a game soundtrack: your game code can call `Asset_set_param("Verb_decay", 6.0)` when the player walks from a corridor into a cavern, without regenerating or recompiling anything. Inside the Skald editor the same mechanism makes knob-turning instant — exposed parameter *values* are masked out of the rebuild fingerprint and applied live through `skald_set_param`, while any other edit debounce-rebuilds and hot-swaps the wasm module (`skald-ui/src/utils/projectSerializer.ts:230-247`; see the flow comment at `skald-ui/src/hooks/nodeEditor/useWasmAudioEngine.ts:1-15`). If you notice that dragging Decay responds instantly but changing a wire takes a beat, that is why.

One naming wrinkle worth knowing before you ship an API. Reverb's Decay and ADSR's Decay are both called `decay`. When two nodes expose the same parameter name, Skald prefixes each with that node's sanitised **label** to keep the struct fields distinct (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1200-1217`). In the fixture, a Reverb labelled "Verb" and an ADSR labelled "Env" produce `Verb_decay` and `Env_decay` (`skald-backend/tests/golden/reverb_adsr_exposure.odin.golden:108-109`). If you leave your nodes unlabelled you get the node id instead, which makes for an ugly public API. **Rename your Reverb node before you export.**

## Try it (hands-on)

You will use `examples/instruments/pads/pad-sequenced.skald.json`. It is a 90 BPM, 16-step patch: a Wavetable pad slowly morphed by an LFO, through a lowpass filter, through a slow ADSR, into a Reverb (Decay 4 s, Pre-Delay 0.01, Mix 0.35), into the Output. The sequencer plays a three-note chord at step 0 and another at step 8, each eight steps long — so a chord change every 2.67 seconds. Slow, sustained material is the right test bed for reverb, and the chord changes give the tail something to bleed across.

1. **Load it.** Sidebar → **Load** → `examples/instruments/pads/pad-sequenced.skald.json`. You will see one collapsed **Pad** instrument node; the reverb lives inside its subgraph.
2. **Start it.** Click **Loop** so it lights up, then **Play**. Give it a full pattern to settle. Listen for the wash that carries over the chord change at the halfway point — that carry-over *is* the reverb.
3. **Open the control.** Click the **Pad** instrument node. The Parameter Panel on the right lists the instrument's own settings and then each sub-node in turn. Scroll to the sub-node headed **Reverb**. You should see Decay = 4, Pre-Delay = 0.01, Wet/Dry Mix = 0.35.
4. **Take the room away.** Drag **Wet/Dry Mix** to **0**. The pad snaps forward and gets noticeably smaller and more synthetic; the chord change becomes a hard cut with nothing bridging it. Sit with this for a loop — it is the reference point for everything else. Now drag back to 0.35 and notice how much *distance* those few tenths of wet signal were buying.
5. **Find the machinery.** Leave Mix at 0.35 and drag **Decay** down to **0.1**. Instead of a tail you now hear a single soft echo about a sixteenth of a second behind each chord. That is Skald's fixed 75 ms delay tap, naked (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:547`).
6. **Hear a comb filter.** Raise **Decay** to about **0.5**. The echo starts repeating and the repeats are still countable — six or seven of them, spaced identically. Listen to the *pitch* colour they add: a hollow, slightly metallic ring that was not in the dry pad. That colouration is the comb's resonant peaks, spaced 13.3 Hz apart because the loop is 75 ms long. Real reverbs cancel this with several mutually prime delay lines and allpass diffusers [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html]; Skald has one comb, so you get to hear the artefact directly.
7. **Walk it up.** Take **Decay** through **1 → 2 → 4**. Somewhere around 1.5–2 s the individual repeats stop being countable and your ear switches from "echo" to "room". Note where that happens for you; it is a genuine perceptual threshold, not a setting.
8. **Prove Pre-Delay is inert.** Drag **Pre-Delay** from 0.01 all the way to **1.0** — a full second, which on any other reverb would produce an obvious one-second gap before the wash arrives. Nothing changes. This is not your ears: the code generator never reads the key (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:538-562`). Put it back to 0.01 and treat the control as decorative for now. It is worth knowing what pre-delay *would* do, because you will meet it in every other reverb you use: 20–50 ms keeps a vocal's consonants readable while still putting it in a room [Source: https://asmr.education/faq/music-mixing/pre-delay-reverb-purpose-settings-mix-clarity].
9. **Break it.** Set **Decay** to **10** and **Wet/Dry Mix** to **1.0**, and let the loop run for four or five passes without touching anything. The pad does not just get roomier — it gets *louder and louder*, then smears into a formless drone where you can no longer tell one chord from the next, and finally goes flat and squashed as the master soft-limiter clamps down (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:2415-2418`). Two lessons in one: a feedback comb at gain 0.95 settles at roughly 20× its input, so a long reverb is an amplifier as well as an effect; and 100% wet on an insert means you have thrown away the dry signal that was carrying the rhythm and the pitch. This is "mud", and it is the most common reverb mistake there is.
10. **Recover.** Mix back to **0.35**, Decay back to **4**, and listen again. The pad should sound like it was recorded in a large room rather than dissolved in one. That contrast — step 9 against step 10 — is the whole skill.

## Why you patch it this way

**Reverb goes last.** The idiomatic chain in every pad example in the repo is source → filter → amp envelope → Reverb → Output. `ambient-reverb-pad.skald.json` runs Oscillator → ADSR → Filter → Reverb → Output; `pad-sequenced.skald.json` runs Wavetable → Filter → ADSR → Reverb → Output. Reverb models what a room does to a finished sound, so it belongs after everything that shapes that sound.

Order genuinely changes the result, and in Skald it can also change whether your patch builds at all:

- **Reverb before the amp envelope** is not merely wrong-sounding, it is a build error. Put an ADSR after a Reverb and the domain split drops the ADSR into the bus, where no voice exists, and codegen exits with a message telling you to move it upstream (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:89-97`). This is Skald protecting you from a bug that used to be silent.
- **Reverb before the filter** *is* legal — Filter is allowed in the bus domain and gets processor-side state (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1139-1141`, `1911-1912`). It sounds different: filtering after the reverb darkens the tail *and* the source together, where the node's own **Damping** control darkens only the tail. Worth trying deliberately.
- **Reverb before distortion** turns a lush tail into a saturated roar, because the distortion is now shaping the wash rather than the note. Occasionally what you want; usually not. Distortion → Reverb is the default for a reason.

**On sends versus inserts.** Outside Skald, the standard practice is to put reverb on an aux send rather than an insert: set the plugin 100% wet on its own return channel, feed several tracks into it, and control each track's depth with its send level. That keeps the dry signal untouched, glues multiple sources into one convincing shared space, and costs one reverb instead of six [Source: https://www.mixinglessons.com/reverb-aux-send-vs-insert/].

Skald's Reverb is an **insert** — it sits in the signal path and its Mix knob is the only depth control. That is not a defect so much as a different unit of work: a Skald patch usually *is* one instrument, and an insert reverb with Mix at 0.3 produces the same signal as a 100% wet send at 30%. What you lose is the *shared* space. If you want two instruments to sound like they are in the same room, you have two options in Skald:

- Sum them with a **Mixer** node inside a single instrument, and put one Reverb after the mixer. Mixer is bus-domain-legal (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1923-1924`), so this works.
- Build a dedicated **effect instrument**: an instrument whose graph starts with an instrument input. Codegen turns that into a `GraphInput` node, seeds it into the bus domain so it keeps running with no voices active, and emits a `<Asset>_feed_input(left, right)` entry point your host code pumps audio into (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:74-80`, `1904-1910`; the generated procedure appears in the golden at `skald-backend/tests/golden/reverb_adsr_exposure.odin.golden:225`). Set that instrument's Reverb to Mix 1.0 and you have a true 100% wet send bus, driven from the host.

## Going further

**Automate Decay from the game, not from the patch.** You cannot wire an LFO into Decay, but you can expose it and drive it from your host. This is the highest-value thing you can do with this node: RT60 is a *room* property, and games change rooms. Ship one instrument, keep `Verb_decay` on 0.4 s indoors and slew it to 5 s when the player enters the cavern. Slew it, do not jump it — the comb's buffer holds the previous tail, and a large instant change in feedback gain is audible as a level step.

**Fake the pre-delay you do not have.** Put a **Delay** node before the Reverb with feedback at 0 and mix at 1.0, and its `delayTime` becomes the pre-delay the Reverb lacks. Set it to 0.03 for the classic 30 ms vocal treatment. Delay accepts a modulation-free `delayTime` parameter with its own 0–2 s range (`skald-backend/core/param_ranges.odin:74-75`) and can BPM-sync (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:510-512`), so you can also lock the pre-delay to a musical division. Be aware this puts a second 96000-sample buffer on the processor.

**Damping is built in — and a filter after the Reverb still has a job.** Real tails lose high frequencies faster than lows, because air and soft surfaces absorb treble. The node's **Damping** control (0–1, default 0, added in packet C5) is a one-pole lowpass on the sample fed back into the comb (`generate_reverb_code` in `skald-backend/core/codegen_nodes.odin`), so every pass round the loop loses a little more treble than bass — the standard Schroeder recipe, and the difference between a synthetic ring and a room. At 0 the comb is exactly the undamped one every older patch has. A **Lowpass Filter after the Reverb** does something different: it darkens the first reflection and the dry signal as well as the tail. Reach for it when the whole space should sound duller, not just its decay.

**Build density with two reverbs in series.** One comb at 75 ms gives you 13.3 echoes per second. Two Reverb nodes in series multiply the echo pattern, and since each one re-injects the other's output, the density climbs toward something less obviously periodic. Keep both Decays modest (1–2 s each) or the compounded feedback build-up gets away from you fast. This is a crude approximation of why Schroeder cascaded stages in the first place [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html].

**Series versus parallel for two different spaces.** Split your source into a Mixer's channels, run one branch through a short Reverb (0.5 s, Mix 1.0) as pseudo early reflections and another through a long one (5 s, Mix 1.0), and balance them on the mixer channel levels. Because they run at different feedback gains but the same 75 ms tap, they will still comb at the same frequencies — but you get independent control over "small room presence" and "big hall tail", which is the split real reverbs give you as ER Level and Tail Level.

**Second envelope on the wet level.** Skald cannot modulate Mix, but it can modulate a **VCA** (Gain node), and Gain is bus-domain-legal (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1913-1914`). Run the reverb output through a VCA and you have a wet-level control you can shape — for instance, a swell that lets the tail bloom only on long notes.

**Mind the memory.** Every Reverb node allocates a fixed `[96000]f32` ring buffer on the processor struct, about 384 KB, regardless of the fact that the DSP only ever reads 3600 samples of it at 48 kHz (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:19`, `1129-1132`, `551`). Four reverbs in a project is a megabyte and a half of mostly unused buffer. On a memory-constrained target, share one.

## Under the hood

The whole node is 25 lines of generator (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:538-562`), and every generated Reverb is the same shape: a **single feedback comb filter with a fixed 75 ms delay**, followed by a wet/dry crossfade. The code comment calls it a "Simple FDN"; a true feedback delay network has several delay lines coupled by a mixing matrix, so read that label as aspirational.

Each sample, the generated code reads the buffer 75 ms in the past, writes `input + delayed × g` into the write head, advances the head, and crossfades (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:551-560`). The interesting line is how `g` is derived from the Decay knob:

```odin
decay_gain := clamp(pow(0.001, 0.075 / max(decay, 0.01)), 0.0, 0.95)
```

(`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:557`). This is the textbook comb-filter RT60 relation. `0.001` is −60 dB, and `0.075 / decay` is the fraction of the RT60 window that one trip round the loop consumes; raising −60 dB to that fraction gives the per-trip gain that lands you exactly 60 dB down after `decay` seconds. It is the same reasoning Schroeder used when he picked g = 0.708 to get a 2-second T60 from a 100 ms delay [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html] — plug 0.1 and 2.0 into the formula above and you get 0.708 back.

The comment above that line records why it exists: Decay is a *time* in the UI but a *gain* in the loop, and an earlier version fed the UI's default of 3.0 straight in as a feedback coefficient, which diverges exponentially (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:554-556`). The `max(decay, 0.01)` guards a divide-by-zero and the `0.95` ceiling guarantees the loop can never sustain or grow without bound.

Two more implementation facts shape what you hear. The buffer is a plain `[96000]f32` on the processor struct — one per Reverb node, shared by every voice (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:1129-1132`), which is exactly what makes Reverb a bus effect. And there is no output normalisation: the crossfade is `in × (1 − mix) + delayed × mix` with no compensation for the fact that a comb at gain `g` amplifies sustained input by about `1/(1 − g)`. That is the mechanism behind the runaway in step 9 of the exercise, and the only thing standing between it and a clipped output is the master `tanh` soft-limiter (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:2415-2418`).

Finally, the preview is not an approximation. Pressing Play codegens your project, compiles the real Odin to wasm, and runs it in an AudioWorklet (`skald-ui/src/hooks/nodeEditor/useWasmAudioEngine.ts:1-15`). What you hear in the editor is byte-for-byte the DSP you export.

## Terms introduced

- **Reverberation (reverb)** — the dense wash of reflections a space adds to a sound after the direct sound arrives.
- **Direct sound** — the sound that reaches the listener in a straight line, before any reflection.
- **Early reflections** — the first few individually distinct bounces off nearby surfaces; they encode the room's size and shape and anchor a sound in space.
- **Diffuse tail** — the later, uncountable mass of reflections-of-reflections that decays smoothly; it carries the room's character.
- **RT60 (reverberation time / decay time)** — the time for the reverberant level to fall by 60 dB after the source stops. Skald's Decay knob is an RT60 in seconds.
- **Pre-delay** — the gap between the direct sound and the onset of the reverb; used to preserve clarity and to imply room size or distance.
- **Wet / dry** — the processed signal versus the unprocessed one. A Mix control crossfades between them.
- **Insert vs send (aux)** — an insert sits in a track's signal path and needs a wet/dry control; a send routes a copy to a shared 100%-wet effect return that several tracks feed.
- **Feedback comb filter** — a delay line whose output is fed back into its input at gain `g`; the building block of digital reverb, and on its own the source of metallic ringing.
- **Comb colouration / flutter** — the hollow, ringing timbre a single comb imposes, caused by regularly spaced resonant peaks in its frequency response.
- **Echo density** — how many discrete reflections arrive per second. Below roughly a thousand per second the ear starts to hear separate events instead of a room.
- **Allpass diffuser** — a filter that passes all frequencies at equal level but delays them by different amounts, used to smear echoes into density without adding colouration.
- **Feedback delay network (FDN)** — a reverb built from several delay lines cross-coupled through a mixing matrix; denser and less coloured than a single comb.
- **Damping** — frequency-dependent decay, so that high frequencies die away faster than low ones, as they do in real rooms. Skald's Reverb has it as a control since packet C5.
- **Bus domain** — in Skald, the part of a generated instrument that runs once per sample on the summed voice output rather than once per voice. Reverb and everything downstream of it lives here.
- **Exposed parameter** — a parameter promoted from a baked-in constant to a runtime-settable field with a clamping setter and a metadata entry in the generated API.
- **Soft limiter / saturator** — a smooth nonlinearity (here `tanh`) that squashes peaks toward a ceiling instead of clipping them squarely.

## Code-vs-intent notes

**1. Pre-Delay is a fully-wired control that does nothing (blocker).** The parameter exists in the type (`skald-ui/src/definitions/types.ts:112`), has a default of 0.01 (`skald-ui/src/definitions/node-definitions.ts:125`), gets a 0–1 s slider plus number box labelled "Pre-Delay (s)" (`skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:237`), and is advertised in the palette tooltip — "Room tail with decay time, pre-delay and wet/dry mix" (`skald-ui/src/components/Sidebar.tsx:273`). It is also written into shipped example patches (`examples/instruments/pads/pad-sequenced.skald.json`, `pad-reverb.preDelay = 0.01`). But `generate_reverb_code` reads only `decay` and `mix` (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:544-545`) and the emitted DSP has no pre-delay stage anywhere (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:549-561`). Moving the slider changes the audio not at all. This directly contradicts standard practice, where pre-delay is one of the three controls that matter most and 20–50 ms is routine on vocals [Source: https://asmr.education/faq/music-mixing/pre-delay-reverb-purpose-settings-mix-clarity]. Note that the codebase elsewhere treats exactly this situation as a defect to be fixed — see the Wavetable comment at `skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:205-207`, "a dropdown that changes nothing is a lie", and the `showIf` mechanism added for the same reason at `skald-ui/src/components/Nodes/ParamNode.tsx:36-39`.

**2. If Pre-Delay is ever exposed, it gets a nonsense range (confusing).** `lookup_param_range` has no case for `preDelay` and no `Reverb` node-type override, so it falls through to the unknown-parameter fallback of ±1 000 000 with default 0.0 (`skald-backend/core/param_ranges.odin:26-38,116-118`). The UI slider says 0–1 s (`skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:237`). An exposed Pre-Delay would therefore publish a `{-1e6, 1e6, …, ""}` row in the `<Asset>_PARAMS` table — no unit, no meaningful clamp. Every other node whose parameter name means something unusual got an override for precisely this reason (`skald-backend/core/param_ranges.odin:22-38`).

**3. Reverb's Decay borrows the ADSR envelope's range (confusing).** `decay` resolves to `{0.001, 10.0, 0.1, "s"}` from the envelope section of the table (`skald-backend/core/param_ranges.odin:60-63`), with no Reverb-specific override. The seconds unit and the 10 s maximum happen to be right for an RT60, so this is benign in practice — but the minimum is not: the exported setter accepts 0.001 s (`skald-backend/tests/golden/reverb_adsr_exposure.odin.golden:260-265`) where both UI surfaces floor Decay at 0.1 (`skald-ui/src/components/Nodes/ReverbNode.tsx:9`, `skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:236`), and the DSP then silently re-floors it at 0.01 via `max()` (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:557`). Three different minimums for one control. The table default of 0.1 s also disagrees with the node's own default of 3.0 s (`skald-ui/src/definitions/node-definitions.ts:124`), though that only shows up for a hand-written JSON with an exposed but value-less `decay`.

**4. Codegen's fallback default for Decay is 0.5 s, not 3.0 s (cosmetic).** When the `decay` key is missing entirely, `generate_reverb_code` substitutes 0.5 (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:544`), while the UI's default for a newly created Reverb is 3.0 (`skald-ui/src/definitions/node-definitions.ts:124`) and the panel slider's fallback is also 3 (`skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:236`). Patches saved by the editor always carry the key, so this only bites hand-authored or externally generated JSON — which would get a room six times shorter than the one the editor would have given it.

**5. A shipped example carries a `size` parameter that does not exist (cosmetic).** `examples/instruments/pads/ambient-reverb-pad.skald.json:6` sets `"size": 0.9` on its Reverb node. There is no `size` in `ReverbParams` (`skald-ui/src/definitions/types.ts:110-114`), no control for it (`skald-ui/src/components/NodeParameterControls.tsx:234-239`), and no read of it in codegen (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:538-562`). The same file uses the legacy `wetDryMix` key, which *is* still honoured (`skald-backend/core/param_utils.odin:113-121`), so only `size` is dead. A reader who opens this example looking for a size control will not find one.

**6. "Simple FDN" overstates the algorithm (cosmetic).** The emitted comment labels the block "Simple FDN" (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:549`), but the generated code is a single feedback comb filter with one fixed 75 ms tap (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:547,551-559`) — no second delay line, no feedback matrix, no allpass diffusion. A feedback delay network is by definition several cross-coupled delay lines, and the density difference is audible: one comb produces about 13 echoes per second where a natural-sounding tail wants on the order of a thousand [Source: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Schroeder_Reverberators.html]. The label is misleading to anyone reading the exported source to understand what they shipped.

**7. No wet-path gain compensation (confusing, arguably by design).** The output line is `in × (1 − mix) + delayed × mix` (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:560`) with no correction for the comb's `1/(1 − g)` steady-state build-up. Commercial reverbs normalise this so that raising Decay lengthens the tail without raising the level. In Skald, raising Decay from 3 s to 10 s roughly triples the sustained wet level as well as lengthening the tail, and the only backstop is the master `tanh` limiter (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:2415-2418`). Contrast this with the Delay node, whose feedback is explicitly clamped to 0.95 at the point of use with a comment about geometric divergence (`skald-backend/core/codegen.odin:529-532`) — the same concern, handled, but neither node compensates the level.
